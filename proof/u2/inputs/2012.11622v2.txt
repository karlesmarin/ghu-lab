[001] Some comments on 6d global gauge anomalies
[002] Yasunori Lee
and Yuji Tachikawa
[003] Kavli Institute for the Physics and Mathematics of the Universe (WPI), University of Tokyo, Kashiwa, Chiba 277-8583, Japan
[004] Global gauge anomalies in 6d associated with non-trivial homotopy groups \pi_{6}(G) for G=SU(2) , SU(3) , and G_{2} were computed and utilized in the past.
In the modern bordism point of view of anomalies, however,
they come from the bordism groups \Omega^{\text{spin}}_{7}(BG) ,
which are in fact trivial and therefore preclude their existence.
Instead, it was noticed that a proper treatment of the 6d Green-Schwarz mechanism reproduces the same anomaly cancellation conditions derived from \pi_{6}(G) .
In this paper, we revisit and clarify the relation between these two different approaches.

[006] 1 Introduction and summary
[007] Given a gauge theory, its gauge anomaly must be canceled as a whole in order for the theory to be consistent.
This imposes non-trivial constraints on the possible matter content.
One notable subtlety here is that,
even if the perturbative anomaly cancellation is achieved, the theory may still suffer from global anomaly,
coming from a global gauge transformation corresponding to non-trivial elements of \pi_{d}(G) ,
where d is the spacetime dimension and G is the gauge group.
It was first pointed out in [ Wit82 ] that such a situation indeed arises for 4d SU(2) gauge theory with a Weyl fermion in the doublet.
[008] In 6d the situation is more subtle, since the anomaly cancellation often involves chiral 2-form fields through the Green-Schwarz mechanism.
The cancellation condition can nonetheless be derived,
by embedding G in a larger group \tilde{G} whose global anomaly is absent.
This approach was originally developed by Elitzur and Nair [ EN84 ] , and
the extension to 6d with the Green-Schwarz mechanism was done
in [ Tos89 , BV97 , ST05 ] . 1 1 1 There are many other works in the late 80s where the analysis in 6d was done without the Green-Schwarz mechanism.
We do not cite them here; interested readers can find them by looking up papers citing [ EN84 ] in the INSPIRE-HEP database. The list of simply-connected simple Lie groups with non-trivial \pi_{6}(G) is 2 2 2 \pi_{6}(G) for classical groups were computed in [ BS53 , Sec. 19] . \pi_{6}(G) for G_{2} and F_{4} were considered as known in the review article [ Bor55 ] and were attributed to H. Toda; a derivation can be found in [ Mim67 ] , in which \pi_{i}(G_{2}) and \pi_{i}(F_{4}) were completely determined up to i=21 . \pi_{6}(G) for E_{6,7,8} were determined to be trivial in [ BS58 , Theorem V] . \pi_{6}(G) for G=SU(2)=S^{3} belongs more properly to the unstable homotopy groups of spheres and was computed by many people, see the footnote 7 of [ BS53 , Sec. 19] and the comments in [ Bor55 , p. 428] .
[009] \begin{array}[]{lcl}\pi_{6}(SU(2))&=&\mathbb{Z}_{12},\\
\pi_{6}(SU(3))&=&\mathbb{Z}_{6},\\
\pi_{6}(G_{2})&=&\mathbb{Z}_{3},\\
\end{array} (1.1)
[010] and the references above found a mod-12, mod-6, mod-3 condition for G=SU(2) , SU(3) , and G_{2} , respectively.
In [ BV97 ] , it was indeed found that the F-theory compactification to 6d only produces gauge theories which are free from the global gauge anomalies.
[011] From the modern point of view,
the anomaly of a theory Q in d spacetime dimensions is described via
its anomaly theory A(Q) in (d+1) dimensions,
which hosts the original theory Q on its boundary.
The anomalous phase associated to a gauge transformation g:S^{d}\to G is now re-interpreted as the partition function
[012] Z_{A(Q)}[S^{d+1},P_{g}] (1.2)
[013] of the anomaly theory
on S^{d+1} equipped with the G gauge field P_{g} obtained by performing gauge transformation g at the equator S^{d}\subset S^{d+1} .
[014] More generally, the possible anomalies of a symmetry group G are characterized by \mathop{\mathrm{Inv}}\nolimits_{\text{spin}}^{d+1}(BG) ,
the group formed by deformation classes of (d+1) -dimensional invertible phases with G symmetry.
This group fits in the following short exact sequence
[015] 0\longrightarrow\mathop{\mathrm{Ext}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Omega^{\text{spin}}_{d+1}(BG),\mathbb{Z})\longrightarrow\mathop{\mathrm{Inv}}\nolimits_{\text{spin}}^{d+1}(BG)\longrightarrow\mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Omega^{\text{spin}}_{d+2}(BG),\mathbb{Z})\longrightarrow 0 (1.3)
[016] where \Omega^{\text{spin}}_{d}(BG) is the bordism group of d -dimensional spin manifolds equipped with G bundle [ FH16 ] .
In particular, the information on global anomalies is encoded in the part
[017] \mathop{\mathrm{Ext}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Omega^{\text{spin}}_{d+1}(BG),\mathbb{Z})=\mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits(\mathop{\mathrm{Tors}}\nolimits\Omega^{\text{spin}}_{d+1}(BG),U(1)), (1.4)
[018] while the information on the anomaly polynomial is encoded in the part
[019] \mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Omega^{\text{spin}}_{d+2}(BG),\mathbb{Z})=\mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\mathop{\mathrm{Free}}\nolimits\Omega^{\text{spin}}_{d+2}(BG),\mathbb{Z}). (1.5)
[020] For 4d theory with G=SU(2) symmetry, the group \Omega^{\text{spin}}_{5}(BSU(2)) is indeed \mathbb{Z}_{2} ,
and is generated by (S^{5},P_{[g]}) where g:S^{4}\to SU(2) belongs to the generator of \pi_{4}(SU(2))=\mathbb{Z}_{2} .
This corresponds to Witten’s original global anomaly.
[021] When we apply this argument to 6d , we encounter an immediate puzzle.
Namely, for G=SU(2) , SU(3) , and G_{2} for which \pi_{6}(G) is non-trivial, we have
[022] \begin{array}[]{lcc}\Omega^{\text{spin}}_{7}(BSU(2))&=&0,\\
\Omega^{\text{spin}}_{7}(BSU(3))&=&0,\\
\Omega^{\text{spin}}_{7}(BG_{2})&=&0,\\
\end{array} (1.6)
[023] and in particular the configuration (S^{7},P_{[g]}) for [g]\in\pi_{6}(G) is null-bordant.
Therefore, the partition function of the anomaly theory on this background should be automatically trivial when the anomaly polynomial is canceled,
and there should be no global anomalies at all for the gauge groups G=SU(2) , SU(3) , and G_{2} .
But the old computations indeed found non-trivial anomalous phases,
so one is led to wonder whether they were legitimate to start with.
[024] The way out is suggested by another set of observations made more recently, in [ OSTY14 , Sec. 3.1.1] and
in [ MM18a , MM18b ] .
Namely, it was observed there
that the mod-12, mod-6, mod-3 conditions for G=SU(2) , SU(3) , G_{2} theories can be derived by demanding that
the factorized anomaly polynomial of the fermions can actually be canceled by a properly constructed Green-Schwarz term.
For example, the non-(purely-)gravitational part of the anomaly polynomial 3 3 3 In this paper, we denote by \mathcal{I} the anomaly polynomial of a theory,
and by \tilde{\mathcal{I}} the part of the anomaly polynomial not purely formed by the Pontrjagin classes p_{i}(R) of the spacetime. of n fermions in \mathbf{3} of SU(3) is
[025] \tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\text{fermion}}=\frac{n}{6}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot c_{2}(F)\left(c_{2}(F)+\frac{p_{1}(R)}{2}\right) (1.7)
[026] and it is always factorized, which is a necessary condition for the Green-Schwarz mechanism to be applicable.
But it is not a sufficient condition.
The instanton configurations of G gauge field correspond to strings charged under 2-form fields,
and their self Dirac pairing is given by n/6 , which needs to be an integer.
This shows that n needs to be divisible by 6 [ OSTY14 , Sec. 3.1.1] .
Furthermore, when this is the case, one can actually construct a theory of 2-form fields with this anomaly,
thanks to the series of works by Monnier and his collaborators [ Mon10 , Mon11a , Mon11b , Mon12 , Mon13a , Mon13b , Mon14 , Mon16 , Mon17 , MMP17 , MM18a , MM18b ] .
[027] As we have recalled, there are now two ways to understand the mod-12, mod-6, mod-3 conditions for G=SU(2) , SU(3) , and G_{2} .
One is from the absence of global gauge anomalies associated to non-trivial elements of \pi_{6}(G) ,
and the other is from the proper consideration of the Green-Schwarz term cancelling the fermion anomaly.
The aim of this rather technical paper is to reconcile these two points of view.
[028] The rest of the paper is organized as follows:
In Section 2 , we carefully study all possible anomalies of theories with 0-form symmetry G=SU(2) , SU(3) , G_{2} ,
and theories with 3-form U(1) symmetry.
This is done by finding the integral basis of the space of anomaly polynomials with these symmetries.
This allows us to deduce the necessary and sufficient condition when the anomalies of fermions charged under G=SU(2) , SU(3) , G_{2} can be canceled by the anomalies of 2-form fields.
The analysis in this section does not use the homotopy groups \pi_{6}(G) at all.
[029] In Section 3 , we move on to study how the derivation in Section 2 is related to the homotopy groups \pi_{6}(G) .
We do this by carefully reformulating
the approach of Elitzur and Nair [ EN84 ] in a more modern language.
The old computations of global anomalies associated to \pi_{6}(G) can then be re-interpreted in two ways.
The first interpretation, which we give in Sec. 3.2 , is simply the following:
if we assume that the perturbative anomaly is canceled by the Green-Schwarz mechanism,
the Elitzur-Nair method shows that there is a global anomaly associated to \pi_{6}(G)=\mathbb{Z}_{k} .
But there should not be any global anomaly, since \Omega^{\text{spin}}_{7}(BG)=0 .
This means that it is impossible to cancel the fermion anomaly by the Green-Schwarz mechanism
unless a mod- k condition is satisfied.
The second interpretation, which we give in Sec. 3.3 , is more geometric:
we introduce a 3-form field H satisfying dH=c_{2}(F) to the bulk 7d spacetime,
mimicking an important half of the Green-Schwarz mechanism.
This modifies the bordism group to be considered in the classification of anomalies,
and then there actually is a global gauge anomaly associated to \pi_{6}(G) ,
which defines a non-trivial element in the modified bordism group.
[030] Before proceeding, we pause here to mention that the arguments in Section 3 which use \pi_{d}(G) only give necessary conditions.
The derivation given in Section 2 , in contrast, gives conditions which are necessary and sufficient at the same time.
In this sense, we consider the latter is better.
[031] Furthermore, the discussions we provide in Section 2 have already been essentially given in [ MM18a , MM18b ] ,
albeit in a slightly different form.
Therefore, strictly speaking, our discussions in this paper do not add anything scientifically new.
That said, the authors were very much confused when they encountered the apparent contradiction that
people discussed global gauge anomalies associated to non-trivial \pi_{6}(G) ,
while \Omega^{\text{spin}}_{7}(BG) is trivial and therefore there should not be any global gauge anomalies to start with.
The authors wanted to record their understanding of how this contradiction is resolved, for future reference.

[035] 2 Anomalies and their cancellation
[036] In this paper, we are interested in the anomalies of fermionic 6d theories with G symmetry, where G=SU(2) , SU(3) , and G_{2} .
To cancel them, we are also interested in the anomaly of 2-form fields, which can couple to background 4-form field strength G ,
which is the background field for their U(1) 3-form symmetry. 4 4 4 The authors apologize that the same symbol G is used in three distinct ways,
for groups in general,
for the specific group G_{2} ,
and for 4-form background field strengths.
Hopefully the context makes it clear which use is intended. We will study when the fermion anomalies can be canceled by the anomalies of 2-form fields,
by carefully studying all possible anomalies under these symmetries.
As discussed in ( 1.3 ),
the anomalies are then characterized by the group of seven-dimensional invertible phases \mathop{\mathrm{Inv}}\nolimits^{7}_{\text{spin}}(X) ,
where X=BSU(2) , BSU(3) , BG_{2} and K(\mathbb{Z},4) .
As \pi_{i}(BE_{7})=\pi_{i}(K(\mathbb{Z},4)) for i<12 , we can think of BE_{7}\simeq K(\mathbb{Z},4) for our purposes.
This equivalence is useful for us, as we will see below, and also has been used in the past [ Wit96a , Wit96b , HTY20 ] .
[037] Since \Omega_{7}^{\text{spin}}(X)=0 for all four cases,
the anomalies are completely specified by the anomaly polynomials,
encoded in \mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Omega_{8}^{\text{spin}}(X),\mathbb{Z}) .
Below we do not worry about purely gravitational part of the anomalies,
which means that we are going to study \mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\widetilde{\Omega}_{8}^{\text{spin}}(X),\mathbb{Z}) .
[038] Over rational numbers, the anomaly polynomials are then elements of
[039] \mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\widetilde{\Omega}_{8}^{\text{spin}}(X),\mathbb{Z})\otimes\mathbb{Q}\simeq H^{8}(X\times BSO;\mathbb{Q})/H^{8}(BSO;\mathbb{Q}). (2.1)
[040] As H^{*}(BSO;\mathbb{Q})=\mathbb{Q}[p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,] where p_{i}\in H^{4i}(BSO;\mathbb{Z}) are the i -th Pontrjagin classes of the spacetime,
Eq. ( 2.1 ) becomes
[041] H^{4}(X;\mathbb{Q})\,p_{1}\oplus H^{8}(X;\mathbb{Q}). (2.2)
[042] Our classifying spaces X have a simplifying feature that
[043] H^{4}(X;\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}\,c_{2},\qquad H^{8}(X;\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}\,(c_{2})^{2} (2.3)
[044] where c_{2} is a generator of H^{4}(X;\mathbb{Z}) corresponding to the instanton number,
whose explicit forms are given in Appendix A .
In the rest of this paper, the Pontrjagin classes p_{i}=p_{i}(R) are always for the spacetime part
and the elements c_{2}=c_{2}(F) are always for the gauge part.
For X=K(\mathbb{Z},4) or equivalently for X=BE_{7} , we also use the symbol G=c_{2}(F) interchangeably.
[045] The preceding discussions show that the anomaly polynomials (modulo purely gravitational part) are rational linear combinations
[046] \tilde{\mathcal{I}}=a\cdot(c_{2})^{2}+b\cdot c_{2}\wedge p_{1}, (2.4)
[047] which has the nice quadratic form to be canceled by the Green-Schwarz mechanism.
This is, however, only a necessary condition, since the Green-Schwarz mechanism cannot cancel anomaly polynomials with arbitrary rational numbers a , b in ( 2.4 ).
We need to find the basis over \mathbb{Z} , not just over \mathbb{Q} , of elements of \mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Omega_{8}^{\text{spin}}(X),\mathbb{Z}) .
They are degree-8 differential forms ( 2.4 ) which integrate to integers on any spin manifold.
We will find in Sec. 2.1 , 2.2 , 2.3 , and 2.4 that they are given by
[048] \tilde{\mathcal{I}}=n\cdot\frac{1}{2k_{G}}\cdot c_{2}\left(c_{2}+\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right)+m\cdot(c_{2})^{2},\qquad n,m\in\mathbb{Z} (2.5)
[049] where k_{G}=12 , 6 , 3 , 1 for G=SU(2) , SU(3) , G_{2} and E_{7} , respectively.
We then use this result to find the anomaly cancellation condition in Sec. 2.7 .
Note that \pi_{6}(G)=\mathbb{Z}_{k_{G}} , but we do not directly use \pi_{6}(G) in the derivation in this section.
[050] Before proceeding, we also note that the results analogous to ( 2.5 ) pertaining to the integrality properties of the coefficients in the anomaly polynomial
were derived in the previous literature such as [ ST05 , KMT10 ] by explicitly computing the anomaly polynomials for free fermionic theories for all possible representations of G .
The symplectic Majorana condition was often not considered systemtically either.
As we will see below, our argument utilizes only a single representation of G ,
thanks to the use of the bordism invariance.
[051] 2.1 With U(1) 3-form symmetry
[052] Let us start with the case X=K(\mathbb{Z},4) .
We will find the dual bases of \mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Omega_{8}^{\text{spin}}(X),\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z} and \mathop{\mathrm{Free}}\nolimits\Omega_{8}^{\text{spin}}(X)=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z} by writing down two elements from each and explicitly checking that they do form a set of dual bases. 5 5 5 We note that \mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}_{2}}(\widetilde{\Omega}_{10}^{\text{spin}}(K(\mathbb{Z},4)),\mathbb{Z}_{2}) was determined in a similar manner in [ DMW00 , Sec. 3.2] .
[053] First, let us take two elements of \mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Omega_{8}^{\text{spin}}(X),\mathbb{Z}) .
One is
[054] G\wedge G, (2.6)
[055] where G is a generator of H^{4}(K(\mathbb{Z},4);\mathbb{Z}) .
The other element of \mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Omega_{8}^{\text{spin}}(X),\mathbb{Z}) we use is
[056] \frac{1}{2}\cdot G\left(G+\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right). (2.7)
[057] There are two ways to show that it integrates to an integer on spin manifolds.
One method to demonstrate this only uses algebraic topology.
Let us first recall that the standard generator \lambda of H^{4}(BSpin;\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z} satisfies p_{1}=2\lambda .
Now, for any SO bundle, there is a relation
[058] p_{1}=\mathcal{P}(w_{2})+2w_{4}\mod 4. (2.8)
[059] Here, the 2 in front of w_{4} is a map sending \mathbb{Z}_{2}=\{0,1\} to \{0,2\}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{4} .
For spin bundles, we have w_{2}=0 , and therefore 2\lambda=p_{1}=2w_{4} ,
meaning that
[060] \lambda=w_{4}\mod 2. (2.9)
[061] With this relation we can show
[062] \int_{M_{8}}G\wedge\dfrac{p_{1}}{2}=\int_{M_{8}}G\cup w_{4}=\int_{M_{8}}Sq^{4}G=\int_{M_{8}}G\wedge G\mod 2, (2.10)
[063] where we used the fact that w_{4} is the Wu class \nu_{4} on a spin manifold.
Therefore the expression ( 2.7 ) integrates to an integer.
[064] The other method is differential geometric, or uses a physics input.
We consider the non-gravitational part \tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{56}} of the anomaly polynomial of a fermion in the representation \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{56} of E_{7} ,
where \frac{1}{2} means that we impose a reality condition using the fact that both \mathbf{56} of E_{7} and the Weyl spinor in 6d with Lorentz signature are pseudo-real.
In terms of the index theory in 8d , the same \frac{1}{2} uses the fact that \mathbf{56} is pseudo-real and that the Weyl spinor in 8d with Euclidean signature are strictly real,
and therefore the index in 8d is a multiple of 2.
Using group-theoretical constants tabulated in Appendix A ,
the anomaly polynomial can be computed and turns out to be
[065] \tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{56}}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot c_{2}\left(c_{2}+\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right). (2.11)
[066] We next take two elements of \Omega_{8}^{\text{spin}}(X) , following [ Fra05 ] .
One is the quaternionic projective space \mathbb{HP}^{2} .
It has a canonical Sp(1)=SU(2) bundle Q , whose c_{2} generates H^{4}(\mathbb{HP}^{2};\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z} such that \int_{\mathbb{HP}^{2}}(c_{2})^{2}=1 .
The Pontrjagin classes of the tangent bundle are p_{1}=-2c_{2} and p_{2}=7(c_{2})^{2} . 6 6 6 The total Pontrjagin class of \mathbb{HP}^{k} is \frac{(1-c_{2})^{2(k+1)}}{(1-4c_{2})} ,
see e.g. [ CG10 ] . The other is \mathbb{CP}^{1}\times\mathbb{CP}^{3} equipped with the element c\wedge c^{\prime}\in H^{4}(X;\mathbb{Z}) where c , c^{\prime} are the standard generators of H^{2}(\mathbb{CP}^{1};\mathbb{Z}) and H^{2}(\mathbb{CP}^{3};\mathbb{Z}) , respectively.
[067] The pairings between these elements are given by
[068] \displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{HP}^{2},Q)}G\wedge G \displaystyle=1, \displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{HP}^{2},Q)}\frac{1}{2}\cdot G\left(G+\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right) \displaystyle=0, (2.12) \displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{CP}^{1}\times\mathbb{CP}^{3},c\wedge c^{\prime})}G\wedge G \displaystyle=0, \displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{CP}^{1}\times\mathbb{CP}^{3},c\wedge c^{\prime})}\frac{1}{2}\cdot G\left(G+\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right) \displaystyle=-1. (2.13)
[069] and therefore they constitute dual bases.
In other words,
the classes (\mathbb{HP}^{2},Q) and (\mathbb{CP}^{1}\times\mathbb{CP}^{3},c\wedge c^{\prime}) generate \mathop{\mathrm{Free}}\nolimits\Omega_{8}^{\text{spin}}(K(\mathbb{Z},4))=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z} ,
while G\wedge G and \frac{1}{2}\cdot G\left(G+\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right) generate \mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Omega_{8}^{\text{spin}}(K(\mathbb{Z},4)),\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z} .
[070] 2.2 With SU(2) symmetry
[071] Let us next consider the case X=BSU(2) .
Our approach is the same as the previous case.
We first take two elements of \mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Omega_{8}^{\text{spin}}(X),\mathbb{Z}) .
One is c_{2}\wedge c_{2} as before,
and the other is this time the non-gravitational part \tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{2}} of the anomaly polynomial for the fermion in the representation \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{2} .
Using the group theoretical data in Appendix A ,
we have
[072] \tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{2}}=\frac{1}{24}\cdot c_{2}\left(c_{2}+\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right). (2.14)
[073] This can also be derived from ( 2.11 ) by splitting \mathbf{56} as \mathbf{2}\otimes\mathbf{12}\oplus\mathbf{1}\otimes\mathbf{32} under \mathfrak{su}(2)\times\mathfrak{so}(12)\subset\mathfrak{e}_{7} ,
which implies \tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{56}}=12\cdot\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{2}} under SU(2)\subset E_{7} ;
see e.g. [ FKS19 , Table A.178] .
[074] We next consider two elements of \Omega_{8}^{\text{spin}}(BSU(2)) .
One is (\mathbb{HP}^{2},Q) as before.
As the other, we take (S^{4},I)\times K3 ,
where S^{4} is equipped with a standard instanton bundle I on it, which has \int_{S^{4}}c_{2}=1 ,
while K3 has \int_{K3}p_{1}=-48 .
Note that S^{4}\simeq\mathbb{HP}^{1} and I is its canonical Sp(1) bundle.
[075] The pairings of these elements are then
[076] \displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{HP}^{2},Q)}c_{2}\wedge c_{2} \displaystyle=1, \displaystyle\int_{(\mathbb{HP}^{2},Q)}\frac{1}{24}\cdot c_{2}\left(c_{2}+\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right) \displaystyle=0, (2.15) \displaystyle\int_{(S^{4},I)\times K3}c_{2}\wedge c_{2} \displaystyle=0, \displaystyle\int_{(S^{4},I)\times K3}\frac{1}{24}\cdot c_{2}\left(c_{2}+\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right) \displaystyle=-1, (2.16)
[077] again guaranteeing that they form dual bases.
Equivalently, we have now shown that (\mathbb{HP}^{2},Q) and (S^{4},I)\times K3 generate \mathop{\mathrm{Free}}\nolimits\Omega_{8}^{\text{spin}}(BSU(2))=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z} ,
and similarly c_{2}\wedge c_{2} and \frac{1}{24}\cdot c_{2}\left(c_{2}+\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right) generate \mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Omega_{8}^{\text{spin}}(BSU(2)),\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z} .
[078] 2.3 With SU(3) symmetry
[079] Next we consider the case X=BSU(3) .
Two elements of \mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Omega_{8}^{\text{spin}}(X),\mathbb{Z}) can be chosen as before.
One is c_{2}\wedge c_{2} as always,
and the other is the non-gravitational part \tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathbf{3}} of the anomaly polynomial in the representation \mathbf{3} .
Under SU(3)\subset E_{7} , we have \tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{56}}=6\cdot\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathbf{3}} and therefore
[080] \tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathbf{3}}=\frac{1}{12}\cdot c_{2}\left(c_{2}+\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right). (2.17)
[081] A dual basis to c_{2}\wedge c_{2} can be taken to be (\mathbb{HP}^{2},Q) as always.
Unfortunately, we have not found a concrete dual basis to \tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathbf{3}} in \Omega_{8}^{\text{spin}}(X) .
Instead we need to proceed indirectly, using the AHSS.
As computed in Appendix B ,
the entries E^{2}_{p,8-p} of the E^{2} page for various X are given by
[082] \begin{array}[]{c|ccccccccccc}X&E^{2}_{4,4}&E^{2}_{6,2}&E^{2}_{7,1}&E^{2}_{8,0}\\
\hline\cr BSU(2)&\mathbb{Z}&&&\mathbb{Z}\\
BSU(3)&\mathbb{Z}&\mathbb{Z}_{2}&&\mathbb{Z}\\
BG_{2}&\mathbb{Z}&\mathbb{Z}_{2}&\mathbb{Z}_{2}&\mathbb{Z}\\
K(\mathbb{Z},4)&\mathbb{Z}&\mathbb{Z}_{2}&\mathbb{Z}_{2}&\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}_{3}\end{array} (2.18)
[083] where the other E^{2}_{p,8-p} are trivial.
From the AHSS, it is clear that E^{2}=E^{\infty} for X=BSU(2) , and indeed \Omega^{\text{spin}}_{8}(BSU(2))=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z} .
We also know \mathop{\mathrm{Free}}\nolimits\Omega^{\text{spin}}_{8}(K(\mathbb{Z},4))=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z} from the explicit analysis in Sec. 2.1 .
Comparing the bases in Sec. 2.1 and 2.2 ,
we find that under BSU(2)\to K(\mathbb{Z},4) , the image of \Omega^{\text{spin}}_{8}(BSU(2)) is 12\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z} .
This means that the extension problem in the AHSS for K(\mathbb{Z},4) is solved as follows:
we have
[084] 0\to\underbrace{E^{2}_{4,4}\oplus\mathop{\mathrm{Free}}\nolimits E^{2}_{8,0}}_{=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}}\to\underbrace{\Omega^{\text{spin}}_{8}(K(\mathbb{Z},4))}_{=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathbb{Z}_{12}\to 0 (2.19)
[085] where \mathbb{Z}_{12} is composed of E^{2}_{6,2}=\mathbb{Z}_{2} , E^{2}_{7,1}=\mathbb{Z}_{2} , and \mathop{\mathrm{Tors}}\nolimits E^{2}_{8,0}=\mathbb{Z}_{3} in the last row of ( 2.18 ).
[086] Since BSU(3) is sandwiched as in BSU(2)\to BSU(3)\to K(\mathbb{Z},4) ,
we conclude that \Omega^{\text{spin}}_{8}(BSU(3))=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z} whose image under BSU(3)\to K(\mathbb{Z},4) is 6\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z} .
This abstract analysis provides a dual basis element to \tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathbf{3}}=\frac{1}{6}\cdot\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{56}} .
[087] 2.4 With G_{2} symmetry
[088] The G_{2} case is completely analogous to the SU(3) case.
One basis of \mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Omega^{\text{spin}}_{8}(BG_{2}),\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z} is given by c_{2}\wedge c_{2} as always and the other is given by
[089] \tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathbf{7}}=\frac{1}{6}\cdot c_{2}\left(c_{2}+\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right), (2.20)
[090] which satisfies \tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathbf{7}}=\frac{1}{3}\cdot\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{56}} under G_{2}\subset E_{7} .
The dual basis to c_{2}\wedge c_{2} is given by (\mathbb{HP}^{2},Q) .
The existence of the dual basis to \tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathbf{7}} can be argued exactly as before,
and the image of \Omega^{\text{spin}}_{8}(BG_{2})=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z} under BG_{2}\to K(\mathbb{Z},4) is 3\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z} .
[091] 2.5 Pure gravitational part
[092] Before proceeding, here we record the dual bases of \mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Omega^{\text{spin}}_{8}(pt),\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z} and \Omega^{\text{spin}}_{8}(pt)=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z} .
Two generators of the latter were found e.g. in [ Mil63 ] ,
which are \mathbb{HP}^{2} and L_{8} , where 4L_{8} is spin bordant to K3\times K3 .
They have Pontrjagin numbers p_{1}^{2}(\mathbb{HP}^{2})=4 , p_{2}(\mathbb{HP}^{2})=7 and p_{1}^{2}(L_{8})=1152 , p_{2}(L_{8})=576 ,
respectively.
As for the generators of the former, we can take the anomaly polynomials \mathcal{I}_{\text{fermion}} and \mathcal{I}_{\text{gravitino}} of the 6d fermion and the gravitino,
which can be found in any textbook:
[093] \mathcal{I}_{\text{fermion}}=\frac{7\,p_{1}^{2}-4\,p_{2}}{5760},\qquad\mathcal{I}_{\text{gravitino}}=\frac{275\,p_{1}^{2}-980\,p_{2}}{5760}. (2.21)
[094] They have the pairing
[095] \displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{HP}^{2}}\mathcal{I}_{\text{fermion}} \displaystyle=0, \displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{HP}^{2}}\mathcal{I}_{\text{gravitino}} \displaystyle=-1, (2.22) \displaystyle\int_{L_{8}}\mathcal{I}_{\text{fermion}} \displaystyle=1, \displaystyle\int_{L_{8}}\mathcal{I}_{\text{gravitino}} \displaystyle=-43 (2.23)
[096] and form a pair of dual bases.
[097] 2.6 Anomalies of self-dual 2-forms
[098] Now that we have discussed the general structures of anomalies with symmetries of our interest,
we would like to study their cancellation.
For this, we need to examine the anomalies of self-dual form fields in more detail.
[099] Let us start by recalling the naive analysis often found in the older literature.
The one-loop anomaly polynomial of a self-dual tensor can be found e.g. in [ AGW84 ] .
In general dimensions, it is given by \pm L/8 , where L is the Hirzebruch genus.
In six dimensions this gives 7 7 7 Our sign choice here is for -L/8 while the fermion anomaly in ( 2.21 ) corresponds to +\hat{A} .
They are the anomaly polynomials for the tensor field in the tensor multiplet and for the fermion in the hypermultiplet in \mathcal{N}{=}(1,0) supersymmetry in six dimensions.
[100] \mathcal{I}_{\text{tensor,one-loop}}=\frac{16\,p_{1}^{2}-112\,p_{2}}{5760}. (2.24)
[101] Furthermore, at the level of differential forms,
a self-dual tensor B can be coupled to a background 4-form \tilde{G} via dH=\tilde{G} ,
which contributes to the anomaly polynomial by \frac{1}{2}\tilde{G}\wedge\tilde{G} .
Then the total anomaly is
[102] \mathcal{I}_{\text{tensor}}=\mathcal{I}_{\text{tensor,one-loop}}+\frac{1}{2}\tilde{G}\wedge\tilde{G}. (2.25)
[103] Note that this does not integrate to integers on \mathbb{HP}^{2} and L_{8} when we naively set \tilde{G}=0 .
Rather, we need to set
[104] \tilde{G}=\frac{p_{1}}{4} (2.26)
[105] for which we find
[106] \mathcal{I}_{\text{tensor}}=\frac{196\,p_{1}^{2}-112\,p_{2}}{5760}=28\cdot\mathcal{I}_{\text{fermion}}, (2.27)
[107] which does integrate to integers on \mathbb{HP}^{2} and L_{8} .
We cannot, however, promote \tilde{G} to be a 3-form field strength, since \frac{p_{1}}{4} is not integrally quantized in general, although \frac{p_{1}}{2} is, as we saw above.
[108] A proper formulation was first proposed by Belov and Moore in [ BM06 ] using the results of Hopkins and Singer [ HS02 ] ,
and it requires the use of Wu structure in general (4k+2) dimensions.
This formulation was then developed in detail in a series of papers by Monnier and collaborators [ Mon10 , Mon11a , Mon11b , Mon12 , Mon13a , Mon13b , Mon14 , Mon16 , Mon17 , MMP17 , MM18a , MM18b ] .
For our case of interest in six spacetime dimensions, a spin structure induces a canonical Wu structure,
and self-dual form fields can be naturally realized on the boundary of a natural class of 7d topological field theories [ HTY20 ] ;
an in-depth discussion can also be found in [ GHP20 ] .
[109] In the case of a single self-dual form field of level 1, the bulk theory has the action
[110] S=-\displaystyle\int_{M_{7}}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot c\left(dc+\dfrac{p_{1}}{2}\right)+c\wedge G\right) (2.28)
[111] where c is a 3-form gauge field to be path-integrated and G is a background 4-form field strength.
It should more properly be written using its 8d extension as
[112] S=-\displaystyle\int_{M_{8}}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot g\left(g+\dfrac{p_{1}}{2}\right)+g\wedge G\right) (2.29)
[113] where M_{8} is a spin manifold such that \partial M_{8}=M_{7} ,
and g=dc is the field strength of c ;
we extend g and G to the entire M_{8} .
Such extension is guaranteed to exist since \Omega^{\text{spin}}_{7}(K(\mathbb{Z}^{\oplus n},4))=0 ,
and the action does not depend on the choice of the extension
since we know that \frac{1}{2}\cdot g(g+\frac{p_{1}}{2}) and g\wedge G integrate to integers on spin manifolds,
as we saw in Sec. 2.1 .
[114] At the level of differential forms, one is tempted to rewrite ( 2.28 ) as
[115] S=-\displaystyle\int_{M_{7}}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot c\wedge dc+c\wedge\tilde{G}\right) (2.30)
[116] where
[117] \tilde{G}=\frac{p_{1}}{4}+G. (2.31)
[118] In this sense, a single self-dual 2-form is forced to couple to \frac{p_{1}}{4} from its consistency,
explaining the observation ( 2.26 ) above.
In this paper, we stick to use the unshifted G which is integrally quantized.
[119] In ( 2.29 ), we can introduce a new variable \hat{g}=g+G and rewrite it as
[120] S=-\displaystyle\int_{M_{8}}\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot\hat{g}\left(\hat{g}+\dfrac{p_{1}}{2}\right)+\displaystyle\int_{M_{8}}\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot G\left(G+\dfrac{p_{1}}{2}\right). (2.32)
[121] As \hat{g} is path-integrated, the first term produces the pure gravitational anomaly of a single self-dual tensor field ‘properly coupled to \frac{1}{4}p_{1} ’,
and the second term is the residual dependence on G ,
which we saw in Sec. 2.1 to be a generator of \mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Omega^{\text{spin}}_{8}(K(\mathbb{Z},4)),\mathbb{Z}) .
[122] The other generator was G\wedge G , which can be realized as the standard anomaly of a non-chiral 2-form field coupled to G both electrically and magnetically.
Combining these two pieces of information,
we find that every possible anomaly of \mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Omega^{\text{spin}}_{8}(K(\mathbb{Z},4)),\mathbb{Z}) can be realized by a system of 2-form fields in 6d .
More generally,
if there are n self-dual 2-form fields, the 7d bulk TQFT has the form
[123] S=-\displaystyle\int_{M_{8}}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\left(K_{ij}\cdot g^{i}\wedge g^{j}+a_{i}\cdot g^{i}\wedge\dfrac{p_{1}}{2}\right)+g^{i}\wedge Y_{i}.\right) (2.33)
[124] Here, i runs from 1 to n , c^{i} are the 3-form fields to be integrated over, g^{i}=dc^{i} are their curvatures, Y_{i} are the background fields of U(1) 3-form symmetry,
and K_{ij} and a_{i} are integer coefficients.
For this expression to be well defined,
we need to require 8 8 8 This relation can be more invariantly stated as follows.
We introduce a lattice \Lambda=\mathbb{Z}^{n} whose pairing is given by K_{ij} .
Then the vector (a_{i})\in\Lambda is a characteristic vector, i.e \braket{x,x}\equiv\braket{a,x} modulo two.
That the coefficients a_{i} multiplying g^{i}\wedge\frac{p_{1}}{2} in the anomaly polynomial of the fermionic part of a 6d theory form a characteristic vector was long conjectured,
e.g. in [ MMP17 ] and finally proved in [ MM18a , MM18b ] .
We also note that an analogous constraint in the case of 3d Chern-Simons theory coupled to the \text{spin}^{c} connection was first noted in [ BM05 , Sec. 7] which was later utilized to a great effect in [ SW16 , Sec. 2.3] .
[125] K_{ii}=a_{i}\mod 2\qquad\text{(not a sum over $i$)}. (2.34)
[126] To see this, we first note
the expression \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j}K_{ij}\cdot g^{i}\wedge g^{j}=\sum_{i<j}K_{ij}\cdot g^{i}\wedge g^{j} clearly integrates to an integer.
Then, we can separately analyze each i .
We finally recall that the generators of \mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Omega^{\text{spin}}_{8}(K(\mathbb{Z},4)),\mathbb{Z}) are G\wedge G and \frac{1}{2}\cdot G(G+\frac{p_{1}}{2}) , and we are done.
[127] If \det K\neq 1 , this theory is not invertible but rather a topological field theory with a multi-dimensional Hilbert space.
Correspondingly, the boundary 6d theory is a relative theory in the sense of [ FT12 ] ,
which does not have a partition function but rather a partition vector .
The system then depends on the background fields Y_{i} in a complicated manner,
and cannot be directly used to cancel the fermion anomaly.
However, suppose that Y_{i} has the form Y_{i}=K_{ij}\cdot G^{j}+\hat{Y}_{i} .
In this case, shifting g^{i} to new fields \widetilde{g}^{i}=g^{i}+G^{i} , one can factor out as
[128] S=-\displaystyle\int_{M_{8}}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\left(K_{ij}\cdot\widetilde{g}^{i}\wedge\widetilde{g}^{j}+a_{i}\cdot\widetilde{g}^{i}\wedge\dfrac{p_{1}}{2}\right)+\widetilde{g}^{i}\wedge\hat{Y}_{i}\right)\\
+\displaystyle\int_{M_{8}}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\left(K_{ij}\cdot G^{i}\wedge G^{j}+a_{i}\cdot G^{i}\wedge\dfrac{p_{1}}{2}\right)+G^{i}\wedge\hat{Y}_{i}\right) (2.35)
[129] where the second line is invertible since it only depends on the background field G^{i} .
It is this invertible part which is used to cancel the fermion anomaly,
and the remaining non-invertible part is irrelevant for our purposes.
Setting G^{i}=n^{i}\cdot G for a single G and integers n^{i} ,
we again see that we can produce an arbitrary element of \mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\Omega^{\text{spin}}_{8}(K(\mathbb{Z},4)),\mathbb{Z}) as the anomaly of a system of self-dual 2-form fields.
In the examples in M-theory and F-theory treated in [ OSTY14 ] ,
the cancellation was indeed done in this manner, where G^{i} were set to c_{2} of dynamical gauge fields and \hat{Y}_{i} were set to c_{2} of the R-symmetry background.
[130] 2.7 Cancellation
[131] After all these preparations, the analysis of the cancellation is very simple.
Consider a system of fermions charged under G=SU(2) , SU(3) , or G_{2} .
From the results of Sec. 2.2 , 2.3 and 2.4 ,
its anomaly is necessarily of the form
[132] \tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\text{fermion}}=n\cdot\frac{1}{2k_{G}}\cdot c_{2}\left(c_{2}+\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right)+m\cdot c_{2}\wedge c_{2} (2.36)
[133] where n and m are integers and k_{G}=12 , 6 , 3 for G=SU(2) , SU(3) , and G_{2} , respectively.
[134] Now, take a number of self-dual 2-form fields and use c_{2} as their background field G .
As we saw in Sec. 2.6 , the form of anomalies which can be produced by a system of 2-form fields has the structure
[135] \tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\text{tensor}}=\hat{n}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot G\left(G+\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right)+\hat{m}\cdot G\wedge G, (2.37)
[136] where \hat{n} and \hat{m} are again integers.
This means that the necessary and sufficient condition for the anomaly of fermions to be cancelable by the anomaly of 2-form fields is
[137] n\equiv 0\mod k_{G}. (2.38)
[138] Here, \pi_{6}(G)=\mathbb{Z}_{k_{G}} for our G ,
but we did not use this in the derivation presented in this section.
[139] We note that the coefficient n controls the fractional part of the coefficient of c_{2}\wedge c_{2} , which comes from the \mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits F^{4} term in the fermion anomaly.
For G=SU(2) , SU(3) , and G_{2} , we have
[140] \mathrm{tr}_{\text{rep.}}F^{4}=\gamma_{\text{rep.}}\cdot\big(\mathrm{tr}_{\text{fund.}}F^{2}\big)^{2}, (2.39)
[141] and we saw in Sec. 2.2 , 2.3 , and 2.4 that a fermion in the representation \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{2} , \mathbf{3} , and \mathbf{7} corresponds to n=1 in ( 2.36 ).
Therefore, the condition ( 2.38 ) can be expanded more concretely into
[142] \begin{array}[]{cl}SU(2):&\displaystyle\sum_{i^{+}}\frac{\gamma_{\text{rep.$(i^{+})$}}}{\gamma_{\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{2}}}-\sum_{i^{-}}\frac{\gamma_{\text{rep.$(i^{-})$}}}{{\gamma_{\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{2}}}}=0\mod 12,\\
SU(3):&\displaystyle\sum_{i^{+}}\frac{\gamma_{\text{rep.$(i^{+})$}}}{\gamma_{\mathbf{3}}}-\sum_{i^{-}}\frac{\gamma_{\text{rep.$(i^{-})$}}}{\gamma_{\mathbf{3}}}=0\mod 6,\\
G_{2}:&\displaystyle\sum_{i^{+}}\frac{\gamma_{\text{rep.$(i^{+})$}}}{\gamma_{\mathbf{7}}}-\sum_{i^{-}}\frac{\gamma_{\text{rep.$(i^{-})$}}}{\gamma_{\mathbf{7}}}=0\mod 3,\\
\end{array} (2.40)
[143] where \gamma_{\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{2}}=1/4 , \gamma_{\mathbf{3}}=1/2 , and \gamma_{\mathbf{7}}=1/4 , and
the summation runs over all fermions in the theory where +/- represents their chiralities.
Some values of \gamma_{\text{rep.}} are collected in Appendix A .
[144] 2.8 Examples: \mathcal{N}=(1,0) supersymmetric cases
[145] Let us provide some concrete examples of the anomaly cancellation.
In 6d \mathcal{N}=(1,0) QFT, fermions charged under the gauge group are gaugini in the adjoint representation and hyperini in the matter representation.
They are in opposite chiralities.
Let us restrict the representations of hyperini and denote their numbers as follows:
[146] \begin{array}[]{cccc}n_{2}&:&SU(2)&\text{fundamental},\\
n_{3}&:&SU(3)&\text{fundamental},\\
n_{6}&:&&\text{symmetric},\\
n_{7}&:&G_{2}&\text{fundamental}.\\
\end{array} (2.41)
[147] Then, the conditions for the perturbative anomaly cancellation ( 2.40 ) are
[148] \begin{array}[]{cclll}SU(2)&:&4\gamma_{\text{adj.}}-4\gamma_{\text{fund.}}\cdot n_{2}&=32-2\cdot n_{2}&=0\mod 12,\\
SU(3)&:&2\gamma_{\text{adj.}}-2\gamma_{\text{fund.}}\cdot n_{3}-2\gamma_{\text{sym.}}\cdot n_{6}&=18-n_{3}-17\cdot n_{6}&=0\mod 6,\\
G_{2}&:&4\gamma_{\text{adj.}}-4\gamma_{\text{fund.}}\cdot n_{7}&=10-n_{7}&=0\mod 3.\\
\end{array} (2.42)
[149] which exactly coincide with the conditions for absence of global anomalies appeared in [ BV97 ] .
[150] 3 Relation to \pi_{6}(G)
[151] In the last section, we deduced the condition when the anomaly of a fermion system with G symmetry can be canceled by the anomaly of 2-form fields, for G=SU(2) , SU(3) , and G_{2} .
Our exposition used a careful determination of integral generators of the group of anomalies.
In the end, we found a mod-12, mod-6, and mod-3 condition, respectively.
[152] Traditionally, the same anomaly cancellation condition was related to the anomaly by a global gauge transformation associated to \pi_{6}(G)=\mathbb{Z}_{12} , \mathbb{Z}_{6} , and \mathbb{Z}_{3} for each G .
However, our derivation in the last section did not use \pi_{6}(G) at all.
In this section, we would like to clarify the relation between these two approaches.
[153] 3.1 The Elitzur-Nair method
[154] Let us first remind ourselves the method to determine the global gauge anomaly of a system under G symmetry, assuming the cancellation of perturbative gauge anomalies,
by rewriting it as a perturbative anomaly under a larger group \tilde{G} whose global gauge anomaly is absent.
This method is originally due to [ EN84 ] .
In the context of the modern bordism approach to the anomalies, it was re-discovered
in [ DL20a ] and further developed more extensively in [ DL20b ] .
The basic idea is as follows.
[155] The global G gauge anomaly may arise due to gauge transformations g:S^{d}\to G belonging to non-trivial elements of \pi_{d}(G)=\mathbb{Z}_{k} ,
which cannot be continuously deformed to the trivial transformation.
The first step is then to embed the gauge group G into a larger group \tilde{G} with \pi_{d}(\tilde{G})=0 .
This means that g can be trivialized within \tilde{G} .
Further, assume that our G -symmetric system Q is obtained by restricting the symmetry of a \tilde{G} -symmetric system \tilde{Q} .
Using this, one can describe the global G gauge anomaly of Q in terms of perturbative \tilde{G} gauge anomaly of \tilde{Q} .
[156] Let us implement this idea, following [ EN84 ] .
We first note that the fibration
[157] G\ \xrightarrow{\hskip 5.69054pt\text{$\iota$}\hskip 5.69054pt}\ \tilde{G}\ \xrightarrow{\hskip 5.69054pt\text{$p$}\hskip 5.69054pt}\ \tilde{G}/G (3.1)
[158] induces a homotopy exact sequence,
which we assume to have the form
[159] \cdots\ \xrightarrow{\hskip 5.69054pt\text{$\iota_{\ast}$}\hskip 5.69054pt}\ \underbrace{\pi_{d+1}(\tilde{G})}_{=\mathbb{Z}}\ \xrightarrow{\hskip 5.69054pt\text{$p_{\ast}$}\hskip 5.69054pt}\ \underbrace{\pi_{d+1}(\tilde{G}/G)}_{=\mathbb{Z}}\ \xrightarrow{\hskip 5.69054pt\text{$\partial$}\hskip 5.69054pt}\ \underbrace{\pi_{d}(G)}_{=\mathbb{Z}_{k}}\ \xrightarrow{\hskip 5.69054pt\text{$\iota_{\ast}$}\hskip 5.69054pt}\ \underbrace{\pi_{d}(\tilde{G})}_{=0}\ \xrightarrow{\hskip 5.69054pt\text{$p_{\ast}$}\hskip 5.69054pt}\ \cdots (3.2)
[160] so that the generator of \pi_{d+1}(\tilde{G}) is mapped to a k -th power of the generator of \mathbb{Z}=\pi_{d+1}(\tilde{G}/G) .
Now, to compute the anomaly under g:S^{d}\to G ,
we consider a G bundle on S^{d+1} obtained by gluing two trivial bundles on two hemispheres at the equator S^{d}\subset S^{d+1} .
The resulting G bundle on S^{d+1} is classified by an element \pi_{d+1}(BG) ,
which is naturally isomorphic to \pi_{d}(G) .
We denote the resulting G bundle by P_{[g]} where [g]\in\pi_{d}(G)\simeq\pi_{d+1}(BG) .
In the modern understanding, the anomaly is the partition function Z_{A(Q)}[S^{d+1},P_{[g]}] of the (d+1) -dimensional anomaly theory A(Q) evaluated on (S^{d+1},P_{[g]}) .
[161] From the assumption that \pi_{d}(\tilde{G})=\pi_{d+1}(B\tilde{G})=0 ,
we can extend this G bundle P_{[g]} on S^{d+1} to
a \tilde{G} bundle on D^{d+2} .
Such an extension is classified by an element [f]\in\pi_{d+1}(\tilde{G}/G) such that \partial[f]=[g] .
Denoting the corresponding \tilde{G} bundle on D^{d+2} by \tilde{P}_{[f]} ,
the anomaly is \exp(2\pi iJ([f])) where
[162] J([f])=\int_{(D^{d+2},\tilde{P}_{[f]})}\mathcal{I}_{\tilde{Q}} (3.3)
[163] where \mathcal{I}_{\tilde{Q}} is the anomaly polynomial of the \tilde{G} -symmetric theory \tilde{Q} .
One can check that the expression ( 3.3 ) gives a well-defined homomorphism
[164] J:\pi_{d+1}(\tilde{G}/G)\to\mathbb{R}, (3.4)
[165] since \mathcal{I}_{\tilde{Q}} is a closed form and its restriction to G bundles is zero by assumption.
Let us now take a generator [f_{0}]\in\pi_{d+1}(\tilde{G}/G)=\mathbb{Z} ,
which maps to a generator [g_{0}]=\partial[f_{0}]\in\pi_{d+1}(BG)=\mathbb{Z}_{k} .
Then the global anomaly we are after is
[166] Z_{A(Q)}[S^{d+1},P_{[g_{0}]}]=\exp\Big(2\pi iJ([f_{0}])\Big). (3.5)
[167] To compute it, note that k[f_{0}]\in\pi_{d+1}(\tilde{G}/G) is the image of the generator [\tilde{g}_{0}]\in\pi_{d+1}(\tilde{G})\simeq\pi_{d+2}(B\tilde{G}) .
In this case, we can attach
a trivial \tilde{G} bundle over another copy of D^{d+2} to the bundle (D^{d+2},\tilde{P}_{k[f_{0}]}) discussed above
along the common boundary S^{d+1} via gauge transformation in [\tilde{g}_{0}] ,
to form a \tilde{G} bundle \hat{P}_{[\tilde{g}_{0}]} on S^{d+2} .
Then
[168] J(k[f_{0}])=\int_{(D^{d+2},\tilde{P}_{k[f_{0}]})}\mathcal{I}_{\tilde{Q}}=\int_{(S^{d+2},\hat{P}_{[\tilde{g}_{0}]})}\mathcal{I}_{\tilde{Q}}. (3.6)
[169] This last expression is often computable,
resulting in the final formula that
the anomalous phase associated to the gauge transformation in [g_{0}]\in\pi_{d}(G) is given by
[170] Z_{A(Q)}[S^{d+1},P_{[g_{0}]}]=\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i}{k}\int_{(S^{d+2},\hat{P}_{[\tilde{g}_{0}]})}\mathcal{I}_{\tilde{Q}}\right). (3.7)
[171] This derivation was originally devised by Elitzur and Nair [ EN84 ] for the 4d analysis,
where Witten’s original anomaly in the G=SU(2) case was derived by embedding G to \tilde{G}=SU(3) .
It was also re-discovered in the context of our recent progress in the understanding of anomalies in the bordism point of view by [ DL20a ] ,
who embedded SU(2) to U(2) instead.
[172] Let us quickly see how Witten’s anomaly is computed in this framework.
The sequence is
[173] \cdots\ \xrightarrow{\hskip 5.69054pt\text{$\iota_{\ast}$}\hskip 5.69054pt}\ \underbrace{\pi_{5}(SU(3))}_{=\mathbb{Z}}\ \xrightarrow{\hskip 5.69054pt\text{$p_{\ast}$}\hskip 5.69054pt}\ \underbrace{\pi_{5}(SU(3)/SU(2))}_{=\mathbb{Z}}\ \xrightarrow{\hskip 5.69054pt\text{$\partial$}\hskip 5.69054pt}\ \underbrace{\pi_{4}(SU(2))}_{=\mathbb{Z}_{2}}\ \xrightarrow{\hskip 5.69054pt\text{$\iota_{\ast}$}\hskip 5.69054pt}\ \underbrace{\pi_{4}(SU(3))}_{=0}\ \xrightarrow{\hskip 5.69054pt\text{$p_{\ast}$}\hskip 5.69054pt}\ \cdots, (3.8)
[174] where SU(3)/SU(2)\simeq S^{5} .
We take the doublet in SU(2) minus two uncharged Weyl spinors as the theory Q ,
and the triplet in SU(3) minus three uncharged Weyl spinors as the theory \tilde{Q} .
By restricting the symmetry from SU(3) to SU(2) , the theory \tilde{Q} reduces to Q ,
since an uncharged pair of Weyl spinors of different chirality can be given a mass
and therefore does not contribute to the anomaly.
The anomaly polynomial of \tilde{Q} is simply \frac{1}{3!}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\left(\frac{F}{2\pi}\right)^{3} ,
which is known to integrate to 1 against the generator [\tilde{g}_{0}] of \pi_{5}(SU(3))=\pi_{6}(BSU(3)) ,
meaning that J(2[f_{0}])=1 .
Therefore, the anomalous phase under the generator [g_{0}]\in\pi_{4}(SU(2)) is \exp(2\pi iJ([f_{0}]))=\exp(2\pi i\cdot\frac{1}{2})=-1 .
In this way, we see that the anomaly theory of a 4d fermion in the doublet of SU(2) detects the generator of \Omega^{\text{spin}}_{5}(BSU(2))=\mathbb{Z}_{2} .
[175] 3.2 6d analysis, take 1
[176] The analysis of Elitzur and Nair was applied and extended to the 6d case in [ Tos89 , BV97 , ST05 ] .
However, the analyses there were not quite satisfactory from a more modern point of view,
since the contribution from the self-dual tensor field was not properly taken care of.
The first objection to their analyses is the following:
we know that \pi_{6}(SU(2))=\mathbb{Z}_{12}\to\Omega^{\text{spin}}_{7}(BSU(2))=0 is a zero map.
How can there be a global anomaly?
The second objection is the following:
we know from Sec. 2.7 that we cannot cancel the perturbative anomaly of a single fermion in \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{2} of SU(2) by that of self-dual tensor fields.
How can we apply the Elitzur-Nair method?
We can combine these two objections into a consistent modern reinterpretation of the old analyses, as follows.
[177] Suppose that the perturbative anomaly of n fermions in \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{2} of SU(2) can be canceled by a combination of 2-form fields, by using c_{2} of the SU(2) bundle as the background of the U(1) 3-form symmetry of the 2-form fields.
We also assume that the perturbative gravitational anomaly is canceled by adding some uncharged fields,
and take the combined theory as Q .
Since the perturbative anomaly vanishes, it determines a bordism invariant
[178] \Omega^{\text{spin}}_{7}(BSU(2))\to U(1), (3.9)
[179] but as \Omega^{\text{spin}}_{7}(BSU(2))=0 , it is trivial.
In particular, the anomalous phase associated to (S^{7},P_{[g_{0}]}) for the generator [g_{0}]\in\pi_{7}(BSU(2))=\mathbb{Z}_{12} should also be trivial, i.e.
[180] Z_{A(Q)}[S^{7},P_{[g_{0}]}]=1. (3.10)
[181] We now compute this anomalous phase by the Elitzur-Nair method.
Taking G=SU(2)=Sp(1) and \tilde{G}=Sp(2) , the sequence is
[182] \cdots\ \xrightarrow{\hskip 5.69054pt\text{$\iota_{\ast}$}\hskip 5.69054pt}\ \underbrace{\pi_{7}(Sp(2))}_{=\mathbb{Z}}\ \xrightarrow{\hskip 5.69054pt\text{$p_{\ast}$}\hskip 5.69054pt}\ \underbrace{\pi_{7}(Sp(2)/Sp(1))}_{=\mathbb{Z}}\ \xrightarrow{\hskip 5.69054pt\text{$\partial$}\hskip 5.69054pt}\ \underbrace{\pi_{6}(Sp(1))}_{=\mathbb{Z}_{12}}\ \xrightarrow{\hskip 5.69054pt\text{$\iota_{\ast}$}\hskip 5.69054pt}\ \underbrace{\pi_{6}(Sp(2))}_{=0}\ \xrightarrow{\hskip 5.69054pt\text{$p_{\ast}$}\hskip 5.69054pt}\ \cdots, (3.11)
[183] where Sp(2)/Sp(1)\simeq S^{7} .
As the theory \tilde{Q} , we take n copies of \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{4} of Sp(2) ,
coupled to the same combination of 2-form fields, by using c_{2} of the Sp(2) bundle as the background of the U(1) 3-form symmetry.
Note that our presentation of the Elitzur-Nair method in Sec. 3.1 applies to this case.
The anomaly polynomial \mathcal{I}_{\tilde{Q}} of \tilde{Q} is n times \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{4!}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\left(\frac{F}{2\pi}\right)^{4} modulo multi-trace terms.
Now, it is known that the expression \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{4!}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\left(\frac{F}{2\pi}\right)^{4} integrates to 1 against the generator [\tilde{g}_{0}] of \pi_{7}(Sp(2))=\pi_{8}(BSp(2))=\mathbb{Z} [ ST05 , Appendix B.2] .
This means that for \partial[\tilde{g}_{0}]=12[f_{0}] we have
[184] J(12[f_{0}])=\int_{(S^{d+2},\hat{P}_{[\tilde{g}_{0}]})}\mathcal{I}_{\tilde{Q}}=n\int_{(S^{d+2},\hat{P}_{[\tilde{g}_{0}]})}\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{4!}\mathop{\mathrm{Tr}}\nolimits\left(\frac{F}{2\pi}\right)^{4}=n. (3.12)
[185] Therefore the anomalous phase associated to (S^{7},P_{[g_{0}]}) is
[186] Z_{A(Q)}[S^{7},P_{[g_{0}]}]=\exp\left(n\cdot\frac{2\pi i}{12}\right). (3.13)
[187] For the results ( 3.10 ) and ( 3.13 ) to be consistent,
we conclude that n needs to be divisible by 12 .
The analysis can be extended to arbitrary fermions charged under SU(2) ,
and we can re-derive ( 2.40 ) as a necessary condition.
The argument in this subsection, however, does not say that they are also sufficient.
Differently put, the method in this section does not directly show that the fermion anomaly can actually be canceled, without the analysis of Sec. 2.7 .
[188] The analysis for G=SU(3) and G_{2} can be rephrased in a completely similar manner
by choosing \tilde{G} to be SU(4) and SU(7) respectively,
so will not be repeated.
[189] 3.3 6d analysis, take 2
[190] The argument in the previous subsection can also be phrased in the following manner.
Let us start by assuming that the perturbative anomaly of a single fermion in \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{2} can be canceled.
Then, we can apply the Elitzur-Nair argument to compute the anomaly under the generator [g_{0}]\in\pi_{6}(SU(2))=\mathbb{Z}_{12} ,
which is the partition function of the anomaly theory on (S^{7},P_{[g_{0}]}) .
The value turns out to be \exp(2\pi i/12) .
The configuration (S^{7},P_{[g_{0}]}) is however null-bordant in \Omega^{\text{spin}}_{7}(BSU(2)) ,
and therefore the partition function should be 1.
This leads to a contradiction,
meaning that the initial assumption is incorrect.
We conclude that the perturbative anomaly cannot be canceled for a single fermion in \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{2} .
[191] This argument still leaves us wondering whether it is possible to have a setup where the anomaly associated to [g_{0}] is actually \exp(2\pi i/12) .
It turns out that it can be achieved by considering a slightly different bordism group as follows.
This is essentially the computation done in [ Tos89 , BV97 ] .
[192] So far, we have considered the manifolds in question to be equipped with spin structure and a G gauge field.
Let us equip the manifolds with a classical 3-form field H ,
which we consider to be a (modified) field strength of the 2-form field B , satisfying
[193] dH=c_{2}. (3.14)
[194] This relation makes
the part of the anomaly polynomial divisible by c_{2} cohomologically trivial, and effectively cancels it.
Let us see this more concretely.
Take G=SU(2) and consider fermions in \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{2} .
The partition function of its anomaly theory A(Q) is given by the associated eta invariant \eta_{\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{2}} ,
whose variation is controlled by the anomaly polynomial
[195] \frac{1}{24}\cdot c_{2}\left(c_{2}+\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right). (3.15)
[196] With ( 3.14 ) we can add a local counterterm in 7d , and consider the anomaly theory to be
[197] Z_{A(Q)^{\prime}}=\exp\left(2\pi i\left(\eta_{\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{2}}-\int_{M_{7}}\frac{1}{24}\cdot H\left(c_{2}+\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right)\right)\right). (3.16)
[198] This combination is clearly constant under infinitesimal variations of the background fields,
and gives a invariant of the bordism group equipped with spin structure, a G gauge field, and a 3-form field satisfying ( 3.14 ).
[199] The G -field configuration P_{[g_{0}]} on S^{7} has a cohomologically-trivial c_{2} , since H^{4}(S^{7}) vanishes.
Therefore we can solve ( 3.14 ) to find H on S^{7} .
The \tilde{G} -field configuration \tilde{P}_{[f_{0}]} on D^{8} can also be augmented with a solution to ( 3.14 ), since H^{4}(D^{8}) is again trivial.
Then the Elitzur-Nair argument goes through,
and this time we actually conclude that
[200] Z_{A(Q)^{\prime}}[S^{7},P_{[g_{0}]},H]=\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i}{12}\right). (3.17)
[201] The classifying space X of a G gauge field and a 3-form field satisfying ( 3.14 )
can be described as a total space of the fibration
[202] K(\mathbb{Z},3)\to X\to BG (3.18)
[203] where the extension is controlled by ( 3.14 ).
Therefore the relevant bordism group for us is \Omega^{\text{spin}}_{7}(X) .
See Appendix B.6 for the details of the calculations;
we do find that \Omega^{\text{spin}}_{7}(X)=\mathbb{Z}_{12} ,
and our modified anomaly theory A(Q)^{\prime} detects its generator.
Again the cases G=SU(3) and G_{2} can be treated similarly,
and we will not detail it.
[204] We now see that with 12 copies of the same fermion system, the global anomaly associated to \pi_{6}(SU(2)) vanishes,
when the manifolds involved are all equipped with a classical 3-form field H satisfying ( 3.14 ).
However, this does not directly imply that one can perform
the path integral over the G gauge field and the 2-form field B satisfying ( 3.14 ).
Indeed, the 7d counterterm in ( 3.16 ) with 12 copies corresponds to the 6d coupling
[205] -\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot B\left(c_{2}+\frac{p_{1}}{2}\right) (3.19)
[206] but \frac{1}{2}(c_{2}+\frac{p_{1}}{2}) is not integrally quantized.
As we know from Sec. 2.7 , we need to use chiral 2-form fields in 6d to cancel the fermion anomaly in this case,
and the subtlety of the chiral 2-forms is not encoded in the classical equation ( 3.14 ).
[207] Before closing, we note that the spacetime structure analogous to ( 3.14 ), given by
providing a 3-form field strength H satisfying
[208] dH=\frac{p_{1}}{2}-c_{2} (3.20)
[209] on a spin manifold, is known as the (twisted) string structure in mathematical literature.
This captures a part of the original 10d Green-Schwarz mechanism, where the fermion anomalies of the form
[210] \mathcal{I}_{12}=\left(\frac{p_{1}}{2}-c_{2}\right)X_{8} (3.21)
[211] is cancelled by the anomaly of the B -field.
Indeed, analogously to the analysis in this subsection,
equipping manifolds with (twisted) string structure automatically cancels the factorized \mathcal{I}_{12} .
It must be noted, however, that vanishing of the anomaly with the use of the (twisted) string structure
does not guarantee that the part X_{8} is integrally quantized,
again as analogously to the 6d analysis.
This needs to be kept in mind whenever we try to use the string bordism to study the anomaly cancellation in string theory.

The only difference is the additional \mathbb{Z} in E^{2}_{8,\ast} elements due to c_{4}\in H^{8}(BSU(n);\mathbb{Z}) ,

[259] \mathop{\mathrm{Inv}}\nolimits_{\text{spin}}^{7}(BSU(n))=\underbrace{\mathop{\mathrm{Free}}\nolimits\widetilde{\Omega}^{\text{spin}}_{8}(BSU(n))}_{=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}}\oplus\underbrace{\mathop{\mathrm{Tors}}\nolimits\widetilde{\Omega}^{\text{spin}}_{7}(BSU(n))}_{=0}. [260] Comparing with the BSU(3) case, we find that one factor of \mathbb{Z} is given by extending E^{2}_{4,4}=\mathbb{Z} by E^{2}_{6,2}=\mathbb{Z}_{2} as before, whose generator is obtained by sending the corresponding one for BSU(3) by the embedding SU(3)\to SU(n) . The other two factors of \mathbb{Z} simply come from H_{8}(BSU(n);\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z} and are dual to (c_{2})^{2} and c_{4} .
Then we see that a basis of \mathop{\mathrm{Hom}}\nolimits_{\mathbb{Z}}(\widetilde{\Omega}^{\text{spin}}_{8}(BSU(n)),\mathbb{Z}) can be chosen to be \tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\mathbf{n}} , (c_{2})^{2} and \frac{1}{2}c_{2}(c_{2}+\frac{p_{1}}{2}) ,
the last two of which can be obtained by pulling back from K(\mathbb{Z},4) .
Applying the logic of Sec. 2.7 , we find that
the anomaly of 2-form fields can cancel the fermion anomaly if and only if the coefficient of c_{4} in the anomaly polynomial vanishes.

[316] Appendix D Recollections of Eguchi-sensei
[317] In this appendix, one of the authors (Yuji Tachikawa) would like to share memories of his late advisor Professor Tohru Eguchi, who passed away unexpectedly in early 2019.
In the rest of the appendix, I would like to use the first person, and I would also like to refer to my late advisor as Eguchi-sensei (江口先生) as I often called him.
[318] My first encounter with him should have been when I was reading the August 1994 issue of a popular Japanese mathematics magazine, “Suugaku seminar”,
in which there was a write-up of his interview of Professor Edward Witten.
It was interesting for me to re-read that article to prepare this appendix.
The interview was dated May 13, 1994, and there was a discussion of the Seiberg-Witten theory in it, which was not even available as a preprint at that time.
I do not think I understood anything back then, as I was a high school student at that time.
But somehow, by a miraculous action at distance, I became a student of Eguchi-sensei several years later, and started specializing in the Seiberg-Witten theory.
It was Eguchi-sensei who advised me to read Nekrasov’s influential instanton counting paper when it appeared in 2002, which became the basis of my career.
[319] In the last year of the graduate school,
recommendation letters by him and by Hirosi Ooguri allowed me
to stay in KITP from August to December 2005 for a long-term workshop.
Despite all his duties in the university, he also attended the workshop for one month in August.
While preparing for my first visit to the US, he suggested me to stay together with him in a large apartment already arranged for him by KITP, since he did not want to live alone.
It is a fond memory that I cooked barely edible breakfast for him every day, and often went to restaurants in Isla Vista together for supper.
He also taught me then what is according to him the most delicious and at the same time the easiest way to eat Avocado,
which is to cut one in half, remove the big seed, and then pour a bit of soy sauce to the resulting depression.
Then all that is left is to scoop the flesh by a spoon.
During my stay there, I worked with Suzuki-kun, who was another student of Eguchi-sensei,
on 6d global anomaly cancellation across the Pacific Ocean using Skype.
As this paper can be considered as a direct sequel to that paper [ ST05 ] ,
I feel it quite appropriate that I dedicate this paper to his memory.
[320] Due to the age difference, Eguchi-sensei was always a father-like figure to me,
including the following unfortunate events in his last few years.
In 2015, I was a faculty member in the department of physics of the University of Tokyo,
from which he had retired and moved to Rikkyo University.
Due to various reasons, I wanted to move to my current affiliation,
and one day I went to Rikkyo University to visit him to ask if he could write a recommendation letter to me,
as he had done countless times before.
I was surprised then that he flatly refused to do so.
I think I now understand how he felt at that time;
he had been in the same department of physics of the University of Tokyo for decades.
Surely he had felt strongly attached to the place, which I was suddenly trying to abandon.
Since then, we met once in a while in workshops and in other places,
but I never felt reconciled since,
just as a father and a son would feel when the partner brought back to the home by the son was not acceptable to the father.
[321] In late 2018, I knew he was sick, but I was never able to make up my mind to visit him in the hospital.
Then came the sudden new of his passing away in January 30, 2019.
I deeply regret that I did not go to see him at least once, before he left this world.
